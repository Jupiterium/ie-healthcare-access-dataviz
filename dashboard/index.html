The problem

When people in Ireland need medical care, two things can stop them: a long wait for public hospital care, or the cost of care. Both are in the news, but it is unclear which one stops more people today, whether Ireland is different from the rest of the EU, and whether what people report follows the hospital waiting list.

Who it is for

Students, journalists and the general public, with no statistics background, who read quickly and want one clear finding. People who plan or campaign for care, such as HSE planners and patient groups, already have more detailed data, so we keep the charts simple and the limits visible.

What we left out

Age, income, insurance, prices and individual hospitals. Two sources and three questions are enough to see the pattern, and about ten yearly points cannot support more. We describe patterns and do not claim causes.

How we show it, and why

Survey: Eurostat EU-SILC

560 rows: 28 areas, 10 years, 2 reasons

The share of people aged 16+ who needed a medical examination and did not get one, by main reason: too expensive or waiting list. A yearly percentage.

Hospital list: NTPF open data

370 rows: 92 monthly snapshots, January 2019 to September 2026

People on the public hospital outpatient waiting list, by how long they have waited. A monthly count.

The two tables measure different things, so we keep them separate and compare them side by side by year. We never join them.

One set of rules

  • Waiting is always orange, cost is always blue, the EU average is grey and dashed.
  • Each chart shows only its main parts: a title, a finding line, labelled axes with values, the data, a legend and a short source.
  • Titles say the finding, and the numbers are checked against the prepared tables.

Who chose what

An AI agent listed options with pros and cons and wrote code. The team chose the chart types, titles, what to leave out and the wording, and checked every number. Each decision and its reason is in our AI Decision Log.

Why these charts

Waiting and cost sit side by side on the same 0 to 3% scale, so they can be compared directly and small cost changes do not look as big as waiting changes. Lines suit change over time, and the EU average is dashed grey so Ireland stands out. The ranking is one value per country in one year, so sorted bars from zero let the reader compare lengths and find a country by name.

What they show

Waiting: Ireland rises from 1.0% to 2.1% (peak 2.7% in 2024) and the EU average from 0.8% to 1.2%, so Ireland is above it in every year. Cost: Ireland falls from 1.5% to 0.6% and is below the EU average from 2020. In 2025 Ireland is 8th highest of 27 on waiting, but far below Finland (7.3%) and Estonia (6.9%). It is above average, not an outlier.

Keep in mind. Ireland's series has a break in 2019 and 2020, which the chart does not mark. The ranking shows order, not the size of the gaps, and it is one year only. The EU average is Eurostat's own weighted figure.

Chart 3 · Question 3, context: the hospital list

The waiting list has grown, and the longest waits have shrunk

3a. The outpatient waiting list, January 2019 to September 2026

People on the list at each monthly snapshot, in thousands. The axis starts at 450 thousand. The line breaks where June 2021 has no snapshot.

Why a line. There are 92 monthly points of one number, so a line shows the trend and the gap. Bars would be too crowded, and a stacked area would hide the shape of the total. What it shows. The list grew by about 170,000 people. It rose to about 654,000 in 2021, fell to about 557,000 by the end of 2024, then rose steeply.

3b. How long people wait

Yearly average of the monthly snapshots, in thousands. 2026 covers January to September.

Why grouped bars. The question is how each waiting-time band changed across years, and bars compare values side by side from a zero baseline. A stacked area would make the 18+ months group, which is the story, the hardest to read. What it shows. People waiting 18 months or more peaked at about 178,000 in 2021 and are now about 50,000, under 30% of the peak, while the 0 to 6 month group grew from about 262,000 to 391,000. The list is bigger, but most of the growth is in shorter waits.

Keep in mind. The list counts people waiting at a date, not people treated, and covers public hospital outpatients only. The data does not say why long waits fell, so we do not claim a reason. The tiny "unknown" band is left out, and 2026 has nine months.

Chart 4 · Question 3, moving together?

The list and the survey rose overall, but not in step every year

The same years in two panels. Each panel has its own scale, and the top one starts at 500 thousand.

People on the outpatient waiting list (yearly average, thousands)

Went without care because of waiting, Ireland (% of people aged 16+)

Why this chart

The two measures have different units, so we use two panels on one year axis. One chart with two y-axes would let us pick ranges that make the lines look tightly linked. A scatter hides time and has only seven points, and bars from zero would make the change in the list look tiny. 2026 is left out because it has only nine months.

What it shows

Both are higher in 2025 than in 2019 (list about 556,000 to 602,000, survey answer 1.0% to 2.1%). Year by year the link is weak: the list peaked in 2021 and fell until 2024, while the survey answer stayed high and reached 2.7% in 2024. They moved the same way in only 3 of 6 yearly changes.

Keep in mind. This shows direction, not cause. The survey covers all medical care and the list covers hospital outpatients only. Eurostat flags a break in Ireland's series in 2019 and 2020, and the 2021 average uses 11 snapshots because June is missing.

What these charts cannot tell you

  • Patterns, not causes. There are about ten yearly points, and two series that trend over the same years look related.
  • Different measures. The survey covers all medical care, and the list covers hospital outpatients only.
  • Small percentages. A difference of 0.1 or 0.2 points can be survey noise.
  • Break and COVID years. Eurostat flags a break in Ireland's series in 2019 and 2020. COVID may affect both sources.
  • NTPF data issues. Repeated rows in 2025 and 2026 are added as split rows, and June 2021 has no snapshot. NTPF has confirmed neither.
  • The EU average is Eurostat's weighted figure, not a simple average of 27 countries.

Sources. Eurostat EU-SILC, hlth_silc_08, and the NTPF outpatient waiting list open data, accessed November 2026. Code and prepared data: project repository.
